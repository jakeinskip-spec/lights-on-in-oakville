Humanities Civic Action Plan · Oakville, Missouri

Keeping South County’s lights on.

Some Oakville streets lose power for days in nearly every major storm. Here’s why it keeps happening, and two fixes that already work elsewhere.

The project in two sentences

A problem you can see from the street. Fixes that already exist.

The problem: Certain Oakville neighborhoods lose power for days during major storms, because old overhead lines run through dense trees with no backup path, and Ameren’s official reliability numbers leave storm outages out.

The solution: Storm-harden Oakville’s weakest lines with stronger poles and smart switches, bury only the worst wooded backyard lines, and require storm-included outage numbers so these streets can’t be overlooked.

Why it happens

Living next to a power plant doesn’t protect you

I assumed being near Ameren’s old Meramec plant meant reliable power. It doesn’t, because the electricity takes four steps to reach your house, and the last one is the weak link.

1 · Generation

Power plant

Meramec coal plant closed in 2022. The new ~$800M Castle Bluff gas plant on the same site runs only at peak demand.

2 · Transmission

High-voltage grid

Big power goes onto regional lines that serve the whole metro, not Oakville specifically.

3 · Substation

Ringer Road

Ameren’s $2M smart switchgear (2023) can reroute power for 10,000+ Oakville customers in seconds.

4 · Weak link

Your street’s lines

When a tree takes down the backyard line, there’s nothing left for the substation to reroute through.

Sources: St. Louis Post-Dispatch, Dec. 26, 2022; Missouri PSC File EA-2024-0237; PowerForward Missouri, Dec. 7, 2023.

Why it hasn’t been fixed

Four reasons the problem keeps coming back

Trees

Trees and overhead wires

Trees are Ameren’s #1 cause of outages. Lines are trimmed only every 4 years (urban) or 6 years (rural), and many hazard trees sit on private property Ameren doesn’t maintain.

Old lines

Old lines with no backup

These lines have poles over 50 years old, backyard spans that trucks can’t reach, and only one path for power (a “radial” line), so one break darkens everyone downstream.

Priority

Small pockets wait longest

Ameren restores the equipment serving the most customers first. A few dozen homes on a wooded line are last by policy.

Government

No city to push for fixes

Oakville is unincorporated. The County Council doesn’t regulate Ameren, and complaints come one house at a time, then fade when the lights return.

After 24 hours, an outage becomes a health and money problem

Oakville has many older homeowners aging in place. Risks climb sharply once an outage passes a day.

Health and safety

  • No heat or air conditioning, dangerous for older people and infants within hours
  • Medical devices stop: oxygen machines, CPAP, nebulizers, power wheelchairs
  • Medicine spoils, like insulin that needs refrigeration
  • Basements flood when sump pumps fail
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning from generators or grills used indoors

Feasible solutions

Two fixes that are already working elsewhere

Ameren already uses the first one in other St. Louis neighborhoods, and Virginia has used the second since 2014.

1

Storm-harden the lines with stronger poles and smart switches

Replace 50+ year-old wood poles with composite “super-storm” poles that resist snapping and don’t drag down the next pole. Add smart switches that detect damage, cut off just the broken section and reroute power from another line in seconds.

Ameren did this in Creve Coeur in 2026 for about 2,500 customers, paid for by the same Smart Energy Plan that covers Oakville. Ameren says its 2,000+ smart switches prevented 330,000+ outages in five years, and 160,000 during the 2025 storms alone. It added 850 composite poles in 2025 and has storm-hardened more than 300 miles of line. (These figures come from Ameren’s own press releases.)

How smart switches work

What a smart switch is

Utility engineers call them automatic circuit reclosers, fault interrupters or FLISR devices (short for Fault Location, Isolation and Service Restoration). They’re automated high-voltage switches mounted on utility poles or inside substations. A smart switch senses a fault on the line, works out whether it’s temporary or permanent, and controls where the power goes next.

With older protective equipment, a crew may have to drive out and replace a part before the power comes back. A smart switch can cut the power and turn it back on by itself when conditions allow. That avoids outages that didn’t need to happen and can bring power back much sooner to the parts of the line that weren’t damaged.

In short

A smart switch turns many would-be outages into a flicker. When a line really is broken, it keeps the outage to the broken stretch instead of the whole line.

Temporary faults vs. permanent faults

A fault is anything that makes current go where it shouldn’t, like a branch touching a wire. A smart switch handles the two kinds of faults differently.

Temporary fault

70–80%of faults on overhead distribution lines, by industry estimates

  • A falling branch that brushes the wire
  • A lightning strike
  • An animal touching equipment
  • Other brief disturbances on the line

The switch opens for a moment to stop the current and put out the electrical arc. If the fault has cleared, it closes again on its own. Customers see a brief flicker instead of a long outage, and no crew has to inspect the line.

Permanent fault

  • A tree lying across the line
  • A broken pole or a downed wire

The fault is still there when the switch tests the line, so the switch stays open. That section stays off until a crew repairs it. This is where the self-healing setup in the demo below matters: it keeps everyone outside the damaged section from waiting too.

Newer approach: pulse testing

S&C Electric’s IntelliRupter PulseCloser checks the line before it recloses. It sends a short, low-energy pulse to see whether the fault is still there, and only restores full power if it’s gone. S&C says this uses about 95% less energy than a conventional recloser. That would mean less strain on equipment and fewer full-power hits on a line that’s still damaged, which can make the whole system more reliable.

The 95% figure is the manufacturer’s own claim, not an independent test. Source: S&C Electric, IntelliRupter PulseCloser.

What a self-healing grid (FLISR) does in the seconds after a fault

  1. A fault hits one part of a feeder, the line that runs out from the substation.
  2. Smart switches along the line sense the disturbance.
  3. The system finds the damaged section and opens the switches on both sides of it.
  4. The switches coordinate with each other over cellular, private radio or mesh networks.
  5. A tie switch closes so a neighboring feeder can power the far end of the line.
  6. Homes and businesses outside the damaged section can have power back within seconds.

The damaged section stays isolated until crews finish their repairs.

What it could cost to cover Oakville

Oakville covers about 15.9 square miles of land. Utilities split feeders into smaller sections so a fault takes out less of the line and power can be restored faster. How many switches an area needs depends on:

  • How densely people live
  • How many distribution feeders serve the area
  • How much of the wiring runs overhead
  • How many trees grow near the lines
  • What equipment is already in place
  • Whether a neighboring feeder can provide backup power

My preliminary research estimates about 0.5 to 1.5 automated switches per square mile, or roughly 8 to 24 smart switches for Oakville. That gives two possible plans.

Option 1

Basic deployment

8–12 smart switches

$350,000–$650,000estimated total installed cost

Puts switches where they protect the most people first:

  • Main feeder trunks
  • Major connection points
  • Critical tie switches
  • Spots where one fault could cut power to many customers

Less automatic isolation and rerouting, but a smaller first investment.

Option 2

Comprehensive self-healing grid

16–24 smart switches

$750,000–$1,500,000+estimated total installed cost

Splits feeders into more, shorter sections across the whole community. Possible benefits:

  • Faults isolated to smaller areas
  • More ways to reroute power
  • Fewer customers affected by some outages
  • Faster restoration for neighborhoods outside the damage
  • More options for utility operators

A bigger, long-term infrastructure investment.

If the lines are underground

Neighborhoods with underground residential distribution (URD) need different equipment. Instead of switches on poles, utilities use pad-mounted automated switchgear: green metal cabinets on concrete pads. My research estimates this can add about 20% to 40% to equipment costs. The real number depends on the existing underground network, the equipment chosen and what installation requires.

2

Bury only the worst lines underground

Burying every line is too expensive. Virginia priced it at about $200 billion. So Dominion Energy started strategic undergrounding in 2014, burying only its most outage-prone neighborhood lines: about 2,000–2,500 miles so far.

It costs Dominion customers roughly $2–$5 a month. Burial runs about $435,000 to $2.5M per suburban mile, and underground repairs take longer when something does break, so it only makes sense for the worst lines.

For Oakville: bury wooded upland backyard lines where trees cause the damage, and storm-harden (don’t bury) flood-prone lines near the Meramec River.

Counterarguments

Answering the pushback

“It will raise everyone’s bill.”

A few dollars a month is less than one $1,000 outage. A new federal grant application, backed by county and state leaders, could share the cost.

“Neighbors won’t want trees cut.”

Remove only hazard trees, survey residents first, and bury lines where trees matter most so less trimming is needed.

“Buried lines flood near the river.”

True. Floodplain lines get hardened and smart switches instead of burial.

“This will take years.”

Start with the worst three lines. Smart switches go in within months.

My ask

Harden or bury Oakville’s three worst lines in the next Smart Energy Plan round, and count storm outages when ranking the worst circuits.

To Ameren’s Archview Division: upgrade [X miles] of Oakville lines for about [$Y], roughly [$Z] per customer per month.

To the Missouri Public Service Commission: require storm-included outage numbers next to the adjusted ones.
